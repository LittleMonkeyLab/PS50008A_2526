DataLab: Background

Author

Gordon Wright

Published

January 16, 2026

Overview

This reading prepares you for the DataLab session. It explains the cognitive tasks you’ll complete and why we’re collecting these data.

What You’ll Be Measuring

DataLab includes six stations measuring different aspects of human cognition and behaviour. Here’s the background on each:

Reaction Time

Reaction time is the interval between a stimulus and your response to it.

  • Simple reaction time: Respond as fast as possible to a single stimulus
  • Choice reaction time: Choose between responses based on the stimulus

Research shows that adding choices increases reaction time — a finding known as Hick’s Law (Hick, 1952).

NoteWhy It Matters

Reaction time is one of the most reliable measures in psychology. Your data will show how consistent (or variable) human performance is, even on simple tasks.

Memory

Code 
# Create a vector
numbers <- c(1, 2, 3, 4, 5)
print(paste("Sum:", sum(numbers)))
[1] "Sum: 15"

The memory task measures how many items you can hold in Working memory.

Miller (1956) famously proposed that we can hold about 7 ± 2 items in short-term memory — the “magical number seven.” Your data will test whether this holds for our class.

Estimation

Estimation tasks reveal systematic biases in human judgement (Estimation bias).

People often:

  • Underestimate large quantities
  • Overestimate small probabilities
  • Anchor on initial information

We’ll explore whether group discussion improves or worsens estimation accuracy.

Confidence

Metacognition is thinking about your own thinking.

The confidence task measures how well you know what you know:

  • Are you overconfident (think you’re right when you’re wrong)?
  • Are you underconfident (doubt yourself when you’re correct)?
  • Are you well-calibrated (confidence matches accuracy)? See Calibration.

Why Generate Our Own Data?

Learning with Real Data

When we analyse data you’ve generated:

  • You understand what the numbers represent
  • You can connect statistics to real experiences
  • The results are genuinely unknown (not textbook examples)

Experiencing Both Roles

Acting as both participant and tester teaches you:

  • The importance of standardised procedures
  • How instructions affect behaviour
  • Why research protocols exist

Preparing for the Lab

Before You Arrive

  1. Watch the preview video — Module introduction (5 minutes)
  2. Read this page — Understand what you’ll be doing
  3. Check your lab allocation — Group A or Group B

What to Bring

  • Your phone or laptop (for Qualtrics data entry)
  • A pen (backup for paper recording)

What to Expect

Time Activity
10:00-10:15 Welcome, setup, get participant IDs
10:15-11:30 Rotate through 6 stations (~12 min each)
11:30-11:50 Form project groups
11:50-12:00 Wrap-up

Working in Pairs

At each station, you’ll work with a partner:

  1. One person is the participant (completes the task)
  2. One person is the tester (administers the task, records data)
  3. Then you swap roles

This means everyone experiences both perspectives.

Group Formation

By the end of the lab, you should have formed your project group (3-4 students).

Consider:

  • Can you meet regularly outside class?
  • Do you have complementary strengths?
  • Will you be reliable partners?

You’ll work together all term on the group research project.
