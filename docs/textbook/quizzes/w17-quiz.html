## Instructions

Test your understanding of research credibility, questionable research practices, and the replication crisis. Understanding these issues is essential for becoming a critical consumer of psychological research.

---

## Part 1: Conceptual Questions

### Question 1: The Replication Crisis

The "replication crisis" refers to:

`r mcq(c("Too many studies being published", answer = "Many published findings failing to replicate in subsequent studies", "Researchers copying each other's work", "Difficulty recruiting participants"))`

A famous replication project in psychology found that approximately what percentage of original findings replicated?

`r mcq(c("Over 90%", "About 75%", answer = "About 36-40%", "Less than 10%"))`

### Question 2: Why Does Replication Matter?

Replication is important because:

`r mcq(c("It shows researchers can copy each other", answer = "It helps us distinguish genuine effects from statistical flukes or methodological artifacts", "It increases the number of publications", "It is required by ethics committees"))`

If a finding does NOT replicate, this suggests:

`r mcq(c("The original researchers cheated", answer = "The original finding may have been a false positive, or there may be important moderating factors", "Science is broken", "We should never trust any research"))`

### Question 3: p-Hacking

p-Hacking refers to:

`r mcq(c("Breaking into statistical software", answer = "Manipulating data or analyses to achieve p < .05", "Using very small p-values", "Reporting exact p-values"))`

Which of the following is an example of p-hacking?

`r mcq(c("Pre-registering your analysis plan", answer = "Running multiple analyses and only reporting the significant ones", "Using a large sample size", "Reporting effect sizes"))`

### Question 4: More p-Hacking Examples

Which is a questionable research practice?

`r mcq(c("Deciding your sample size before collecting data", answer = "Stopping data collection as soon as you get p < .05", "Reporting all your analyses, significant or not", "Using pre-registered hypotheses"))`

Selectively removing outliers until your result becomes significant is:

`r mcq(c("Good data cleaning", "Standard practice", answer = "A form of p-hacking", "Always necessary"))`

### Question 5: HARKing

HARKing stands for:

`r mcq(c("High Achievement Research Knowledge", answer = "Hypothesising After Results are Known", "Honest And Reliable Knowledge", "Helpful Analyses for Research Knowledge"))`

HARKing is problematic because:

`r mcq(c("It takes too long", answer = "It presents exploratory findings as if they were predicted, inflating false positive rates", "It requires too many participants", "Hypotheses are unnecessary"))`

### Question 6: Publication Bias

Publication bias means:

`r mcq(c("All studies get published equally", answer = "Studies with significant results are more likely to be published than non-significant ones", "Journals are biased against psychology", "Too many studies are published"))`

Publication bias contributes to the replication crisis because:

`r mcq(c("It encourages more research", answer = "The published literature over-represents findings that may be false positives", "It makes journals too selective", "Non-significant results are always wrong"))`

## Part 2: Interpretation Questions

### Question 7: Identifying Questionable Practices

A researcher reports: "After extensive data analysis, we discovered an unexpected but significant relationship between X and Y."

What concern does this raise?

`r mcq(c("The finding is definitely wrong", answer = "Possible HARKing - the finding may be exploratory but presented as if predicted", "The analysis was too extensive", "The relationship is too strong"))`

### Question 8: Evaluating Research Practices

Which researcher is following best practices?

`r mcq(c("Researcher A: Removes outliers until getting p < .05", "Researcher B: Only reports significant findings from many analyses", answer = "Researcher C: Pre-registered hypothesis and reports all results regardless of significance", "Researcher D: Changes hypothesis after seeing preliminary data"))`

### Question 9: The "File Drawer Problem"

The "file drawer problem" refers to:

`r mcq(c("Researchers losing their data", answer = "Non-significant studies remaining unpublished (in file drawers) while significant ones get published", "Too much data storage needed", "Poor file organisation"))`

This contributes to:

`r mcq(c("Better science", answer = "A biased view of what effects actually exist", "Faster publication", "More efficient research"))`

### Question 10: Statistical Power

Low statistical power increases the risk of:

`r mcq(c("Finding an effect when none exists", answer = "Missing a real effect (false negative) AND inflated effect sizes when effects are found", "Too many significant results", "Ethical violations"))`

Studies with low power that do find significant effects often:

`r mcq(c("Are more reliable", answer = "Report inflated effect sizes (winner's curse)", "Have smaller p-values", "Are more likely to replicate"))`

## Part 3: Application Questions

### Question 11: Improving Research Credibility

Pre-registration helps prevent:

`r mcq(c("Data collection errors", answer = "HARKing and p-hacking by committing to analyses before seeing data", "Participant dropout", "Plagiarism"))`

Open data and open materials help by:

`r mcq(c("Making research faster", answer = "Allowing others to verify findings and detect errors", "Reducing costs", "Guaranteeing replication"))`

### Question 12: Evaluating Claims

A study claims to have found a significant effect (p = .049) after testing 20 different hypotheses.

Your main concern should be:

`r mcq(c("The effect is very strong", "The sample was too large", answer = "Multiple comparisons increase the chance of a false positive - one in 20 tests would be 'significant' by chance", "The p-value is too small"))`

Which features increase your confidence in a finding?

Successful independent replication: `r torf(TRUE)`

## Part 4: MCQ Exam Practice

::: {.webex-check .webex-box}

### Scenario 1: Evaluating a Study

A researcher tests whether a new teaching method improves exam scores. They collect data from 20 students but find p = .08. They then collect 10 more students, re-analyse, and now find p = .04. They report only the final analysis.

1. **What questionable practice is this?** `r mcq(c("HARKing", answer = "Optional stopping / peeking at data", "Pre-registration", "Replication"))`

2. **Why is this problematic?** `r mcq(c("30 students is too few", answer = "Adding participants until reaching significance inflates false positive rate", "The effect is too small", "They should have used more students initially"))`

3. **A better approach would be:** `r mcq(c("Never collect more data", answer = "Pre-register the sample size and stick to it", "Stop at 20 students", "Use a one-tailed test"))`

:::

::: {.webex-check .webex-box}

### Scenario 2: Interpreting Replication Failure

Study A (original, n=30) finds: d = 0.85, p = .02
Study B (replication, n=200) finds: d = 0.12, p = .31

1. **The replication found:** `r mcq(c("A stronger effect", "The same effect", answer = "A much smaller, non-significant effect", "A negative effect"))`

2. **Which study's effect size is likely more accurate?** `r mcq(c("Study A (original)", answer = "Study B (larger sample, more precise)", "They are equally accurate", "Neither"))`

3. **The original study's large effect size may have been due to:** `r mcq(c("Better methodology", answer = "Winner's curse (inflated effects in underpowered significant findings)", "More careful data collection", "Superior participants"))`

4. **What should we conclude?** `r mcq(c("The original study was fraudulent", answer = "The effect may be smaller than originally reported, or not reliable", "Replications are always wrong", "Never trust psychology research"))`

:::

::: {.webex-check .webex-box}

### Scenario 3: Identifying Best Practices

A researcher plans to test whether exercise improves mood. Before collecting any data, they:
- Register their hypothesis on OSF
- Specify they will collect exactly 100 participants
- State they will use a paired t-test
- Commit to reporting all results regardless of significance

1. **This is an example of:** `r mcq(c("HARKing", "p-hacking", answer = "Pre-registration", "Publication bias"))`

2. **This practice helps prevent:** `r mcq(c("Participant dropout", answer = "Changing the analysis plan based on the data", "Recruitment difficulties", "Ethical violations"))`

3. **If the final p-value is .07, the researcher should:** `r mcq(c("Collect more data until p < .05", "Change to a one-tailed test", answer = "Report the non-significant result as planned", "Not publish the study"))`

4. **This researcher is demonstrating:** `r mcq(c("Poor planning", answer = "Commitment to transparency and reducing bias", "Unnecessary rigidity", "Overcaution"))`

:::

::: {.webex-check .webex-box}

### Scenario 4: Red Flags in Research

A published paper reports finding significant effects for 18 out of 20 tested relationships (all p < .05).

1. **Is this pattern suspicious?** `r torf(TRUE)`

2. **Why?** `r mcq(c("18 significant results is impossible", answer = "This pattern is unlikely without selective reporting or p-hacking", "Too many relationships were tested", "The effects must be very large"))`

3. **What would increase your confidence in these findings?**
`r mcq(c("If they reported even more significant results", answer = "If the study was pre-registered and the analysis plan showed these specific tests were planned", "If the sample size was smaller", "If only significant results were discussed"))`

:::

Before moving on, make sure you can:

- [ ] Explain what the replication crisis is and why it matters
- [ ] Define p-hacking and give examples
- [ ] Define HARKing and explain why it's problematic
- [ ] Explain publication bias and the file drawer problem
- [ ] Describe how pre-registration improves research credibility
- [ ] Identify red flags in research reporting
- [ ] Explain the relationship between statistical power and replication
- [ ] Describe what makes research findings more credible

::: {.callout-important}
## Becoming a Critical Consumer
Understanding these issues helps you evaluate research critically. When reading a study, ask: Was it pre-registered? Are all analyses reported? Is the sample size adequate? Has it been replicated? These questions help distinguish robust findings from potential false positives.
:::
