Week 12 Reading

Distributions & Variability

Author

Gordon Wright

Published

January 19, 2026

Overview

This reading introduces the fundamental tools for describing data: measures of central tendency, measures of variability, and distributions. These concepts form the foundation for everything else in statistics.

Why Summarise Data?

Imagine you collected reaction times from 100 participants. You now have 100 numbers. What do you do with them?

Raw data is overwhelming. We need ways to:

  1. Compress the data into meaningful summaries
  2. Compare groups or conditions
  3. Communicate findings to others
  4. Prepare for statistical tests

Descriptive statistics provide these summaries without losing essential information about what the data is telling us.

Central Tendency: Where Is the Centre?

Central tendency describes the “typical” or “central” value in a dataset. There are three main measures:

The Mean

The mean is the arithmetic average — add up all values and divide by how many there are.

\[\bar{x} = \frac{\sum x_i}{n}\]

Where:

  • \(\bar{x}\) is the mean (pronounced “x-bar”)
  • \(\sum\) means “sum of”
  • \(x_i\) represents each individual value
  • \(n\) is the number of values

Example: Reaction times (in ms): 245, 312, 278, 298, 267

\[\bar{x} = \frac{245 + 312 + 278 + 298 + 267}{5} = \frac{1400}{5} = 280\text{ms}\]

Strengths:

  • Uses all the data
  • Mathematically useful for further calculations
  • Most people understand it

Weaknesses:

  • Sensitive to extreme values (outliers)
  • Can be misleading with skewed data

The Median

The median is the middle value when data is arranged in order.

Steps:

  1. Sort values from lowest to highest
  2. Find the middle value
  3. If there’s an even number of values, average the two middle ones

Example: Same reaction times sorted: 245, 267, 278, 298, 312

The median is 278ms (the middle value).

With even numbers: 245, 267, 278, 298, 312, 340

Median = (278 + 298) / 2 = 288ms

Strengths:

  • Not affected by outliers
  • Better for skewed distributions
  • Meaningful for ordinal data

Weaknesses:

  • Doesn’t use all information
  • Less useful for calculations

The Mode

The mode is the most frequently occurring value.

Example: Memory scores: 5, 6, 7, 7, 7, 8, 9

Mode = 7 (appears three times)

Strengths:

  • Works with categorical data
  • Easy to identify
  • Shows what’s most common

Weaknesses:

  • May not exist (all values unique)
  • May have multiple modes (bimodal, multimodal)
  • Ignores most of the data

When to Use Which?

Measure Best For Avoid When
Mean Normally distributed, continuous data Outliers present, skewed data
Median Skewed data, ordinal data You need mathematical precision
Mode Categorical data, finding typical category Continuous data with no repeats
TipRule of Thumb

If mean and median are similar, the data is roughly symmetric. If they differ substantially, the data is likely skewed.

Variability: How Spread Out Is the Data?

Two datasets can have the same mean but look very different:

  • Dataset A: 48, 49, 50, 51, 52 (Mean = 50)
  • Dataset B: 10, 30, 50, 70, 90 (Mean = 50)

Variability tells us how spread out the values are around the centre.

Range

The range is the simplest measure: maximum minus minimum.

\[\text{Range} = x_{max} - x_{min}\]

Dataset A: Range = 52 - 48 = 4

Dataset B: Range = 90 - 10 = 80

Strengths: Easy to calculate and understand

Weaknesses: Based on only two values; sensitive to outliers

Variance

Variance measures the average of squared deviations from the mean.

For a sample:

\[s^2 = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}{n-1}\]

Steps:

  1. Find the mean
  2. Subtract the mean from each value (deviation)
  3. Square each deviation
  4. Sum the squared deviations
  5. Divide by (n-1)

Example: Reaction times: 245, 312, 278, 298, 267 (Mean = 280)

Value Deviation Squared
245 -35 1225
312 +32 1024
278 -2 4
298 +18 324
267 -13 169
Sum 0 2746

Variance = 2746 / (5-1) = 686.5 ms²

NoteWhy n-1?

We divide by (n-1), not n, because we’re estimating the population variance from a sample. This “degrees of freedom” correction makes our estimate unbiased. You’ll understand this better when we cover inference.

Standard Deviation

The standard deviation is the square root of variance:

\[s = \sqrt{s^2}\]

For our example: SD = √686.5 = 26.2ms

Why take the square root? Variance is in squared units (ms²), which isn’t meaningful. Standard deviation returns to the original units (ms).

Interpreting SD:

  • Roughly 68% of data falls within 1 SD of the mean
  • Roughly 95% falls within 2 SDs
  • Roughly 99.7% falls within 3 SDs

For our reaction times: Mean = 280ms, SD = 26.2ms

  • About 68% of times are between 254ms and 306ms
  • About 95% are between 228ms and 332ms

Comparing Variability Measures

Measure Formula Units Use
Range Max - Min Same as data Quick check
Variance Average squared deviation Squared units Calculations
SD √Variance Same as data Reporting

Distributions: The Shape of Data

A distribution shows how values are spread across their possible range. We can visualise distributions using histograms or density plots.

The Normal Distribution

The normal distribution (or Gaussian distribution) is symmetric and bell-shaped:

  • Most values cluster around the mean
  • Fewer values appear as you move away from the mean
  • The distribution is perfectly symmetric

Properties of normal distributions:

  • Mean = Median = Mode (all at the centre)
  • Defined by just two parameters: mean (location) and SD (spread)
  • Many natural phenomena approximate normal distributions

Skewed Distributions

Positive skew (right skew): Tail extends to the right

  • Most values are low, with some very high values
  • Mean > Median
  • Example: Income (most people earn moderate amounts; few earn very high)

Negative skew (left skew): Tail extends to the left

  • Most values are high, with some very low values
  • Mean < Median
  • Example: Age at retirement (most retire around 65; some retire much earlier)
TipMemory Aid

The tail points in the direction of the skew. Positive skew = tail pointing to positive (right) side.

Bimodal Distributions

Bimodal distributions have two peaks, suggesting two subgroups in the data.

Example: Reaction times might be bimodal if:

  • One group understood the task immediately
  • Another group took longer to learn

Identifying Distribution Shape

To identify shape, look for:

  1. Symmetry: Is there a mirror image around the centre?
  2. Tails: Where do extreme values appear?
  3. Peaks: How many centres are there?
Shape Mean vs Median Visual
Normal Mean ≈ Median Symmetric bell
Positive skew Mean > Median Tail to right
Negative skew Mean < Median Tail to left
Bimodal Depends Two peaks

Why Distribution Shape Matters

Distribution shape affects:

  1. Which central tendency to report: Use median for skewed data
  2. Which statistical tests to use: Some tests assume normality
  3. How to interpret the data: Skew suggests something interesting is happening
  4. Outlier detection: What counts as “unusual” depends on the distribution

Applying This to DataLab

Your DataLab data provides real examples:

Reaction Times

  • Likely right-skewed (some slow responses, but can’t go below zero)
  • Should we use mean or median?
  • What does the SD tell us about consistency?

Memory Scores

  • Might be roughly normal (most people near average, fewer at extremes)
  • Is there a ceiling effect (many people at maximum)?
  • Does variability differ between tasks?

Estimation

  • Likely skewed (errors often in one direction)
  • Compare individual vs group estimates
  • Which is more variable?

Reporting Descriptives in APA Format

When writing up results, report descriptives clearly:

Format: (M = value, SD = value)

Examples:

Participants’ simple reaction times were relatively fast (M = 280ms, SD = 26.2ms).

Memory performance was moderate (M = 6.4 words, SD = 1.8).

For comparing groups:

Group A (M = 285ms, SD = 24.1ms) responded faster than Group B (M = 312ms, SD = 31.7ms).

ImportantAlways Report Both

The mean without SD is incomplete. Always report measures of both central tendency AND variability.

Summary

Concept Key Point
Mean Arithmetic average — sensitive to outliers
Median Middle value — robust to outliers
Mode Most frequent — works for categories
Range Max minus min — simple but limited
Variance Average squared deviation — for calculations
SD Square root of variance — for reporting
Normal Symmetric, bell-shaped
Skew Asymmetric — positive (right) or negative (left)
