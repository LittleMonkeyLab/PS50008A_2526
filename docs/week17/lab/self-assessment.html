Week 17 Self-Assessment

MCQ & EDS Practice

Author

Gordon Wright

Published

4 March 2026

Overview

This self-assessment is for your own revision for the MCQ Exam and the EDS (Experimental Design & Statistics) assignment. The lab session this week is for group project work (Methods section); use this page in your own time to practise assessment-style questions.

  1. Part A: MCQ-style questions — Design, IV/DV, hypotheses, test choice, p-values, correlation, and general concepts (mirrors the MCQ exam format).
  2. Part B: Error detection — Wrong test type, CI misinterpretation, direction errors, df errors (mirrors EDS Section A, especially Q6).
NoteFormative assessment

Use this to prepare for the MCQ Exam (11 March) and the EDS Assignment (due 20 March). The lecture covers the content; this gives you practice.

Part A: MCQ-Style Questions

A1: Design and Variables

Scenario: A researcher randomly assigned 60 students to either handwrite notes or type notes during a lecture. One week later, everyone took a test on the lecture content.

  • What design is this?
  • What is the IV (and its levels)?
  • What is the DV?
  • What statistical test should be used?
TipAnswers

Design: Between-participants (independent groups). IV: Note-taking method (handwriting vs typing). DV: Test score on lecture content. Test: Independent-samples t-test.

A2: Correlational Design

Scenario: A researcher measured weekly study hours and end-of-year grades for 80 students. A significant positive correlation was found (p < .01).

  • What design is this?
  • Is there an independent variable?
  • What does “significant positive correlation” mean here?
  • What test was used?
TipAnswers

Design: Correlational. There is no IV — both variables are measured. Positive correlation: more study hours is associated with higher grades. Test: Pearson’s r.

A3: Within-Participants and Counterbalancing

Scenario: A researcher had 28 participants complete a spot-the-difference puzzle once with gum and once without; order was counterbalanced.

  • What design?
  • Why counterbalance?
  • What test?
TipAnswers

Design: Within-participants (repeated measures). Counterbalancing: To control for practice or fatigue (order) effects. Test: Paired-samples t-test.

A4: Hypotheses

  • What is a two-tailed hypothesis? (Give an example for a study comparing two conditions.)
  • What is a one-tailed hypothesis? (Give an example for a correlation study.)
TipAnswers

Two-tailed: Predicts a significant difference without specifying direction (e.g. “There will be a significant difference in test scores between the two groups”). One-tailed: Predicts direction (e.g. “There will be a significant negative correlation between social media use and self-esteem”).

A5: p-Values and Significance

  • If p = .03, is the result significant at α = .05? Do we reject the null hypothesis?
  • If p > .05, what do we conclude?
  • If p < .01, how often might this pattern occur by chance?
TipAnswers

p = .03: Yes, significant; reject the null. p > .05: Not significant; do not reject the null. p < .01: Less than or equal to 1 time in 100 by chance.

A6: Correlation and Causation

  • Can a researcher conclude that X causes Y from a significant correlation alone? Why or why not?
TipAnswer

No. Correlation does not imply causation. Confounding variables, direction of cause, and third variables could explain the association. Experimental designs are needed to support causal claims.

A7: General Concepts

  • What is operationalisation? (Example: “intelligence”.)
  • What helps most for replication — hypothesis, procedure/materials, or participant names?
  • What is a confounding variable? (Example: caffeine group tested in morning, control in evening.)
  • What does the null hypothesis state?
  • The larger group we want to generalise to is called the ___?
TipAnswers

Operationalisation: Defining a construct by how it is measured (e.g. intelligence = score on an IQ test). Replication: Detailed procedure and materials. Confounding: An uncontrolled variable that co-varies with the IV (e.g. time of day). Null: No significant difference or effect in the population. Population.

Part B: Error Detection (EDS Q6 Style)

Exercise 1: Paired vs Independent

A researcher studied whether caffeine improves memory. They gave Group A (n = 20) caffeine and Group B (n = 20) a placebo, then tested memory. The researcher ran:

> t.test(group_a_scores, group_b_scores, paired = TRUE)

    Paired t-test

data:  group_a_scores and group_b_scores
t = 2.14, df = 19, p-value = 0.045

a) What error did the researcher make?

TipShow Answer

The researcher used paired = TRUE when they should have used paired = FALSE (independent samples t-test). Group A and Group B are different people - this is a between-subjects design.

b) Why is this wrong?

TipShow Answer
  • A paired test assumes each score in Group A is meaningfully matched to a score in Group B
  • With different participants, there’s no logical pairing
  • The test is treating unrelated scores as if they come from the same person measured twice
  • The degrees of freedom are wrong (should be ~38, not 19)

c) How might this affect the conclusions?

TipShow Answer
  • The test creates arbitrary pairings between unrelated participants
  • This can inflate or deflate the t-value depending on the random pairing
  • The p-value may be incorrect (could be a false positive or false negative)
  • The researcher might conclude there IS an effect when there isn’t, or miss a real effect

Exercise 2: Independent When Should Be Paired

A researcher measured participants’ anxiety before AND after a relaxation intervention:

> t.test(pre_anxiety, post_anxiety, paired = FALSE)

    Welch Two Sample t-test

data:  pre_anxiety and post_anxiety
t = 1.67, df = 37.4, p-value = 0.103

a) What error did the researcher make?

TipShow Answer

The researcher used an independent t-test (paired = FALSE) when they should have used a paired t-test (paired = TRUE). The same participants were measured before and after.

b) What is the consequence of this error?

TipShow Answer
  • The paired test is usually more powerful because it controls for individual differences
  • By using an independent test, the researcher is treating pre and post scores as if they came from different people
  • This adds variance (individual differences) that the paired test would remove
  • Result: The test is less sensitive - may fail to detect a real effect (Type II error)
  • The df is also wrong (should be n-1 = ~19, not ~38)

Part 2: Misinterpreting Output

Exercise 3: Confidence Interval Misinterpretation

A student reports: “The 95% CI was [-3.2, 8.7], so the result was statistically significant at p < .05.”

a) What is wrong with this interpretation?

TipShow Answer

The student’s interpretation is incorrect. The CI [-3.2, 8.7] includes zero, which means: - The true difference could be negative, zero, or positive - We cannot be confident there’s a real difference - The result is NOT significant at p < .05 - When CI includes zero, p > .05

b) What should the correct interpretation be?

TipShow Answer

“The 95% CI [-3.2, 8.7] includes zero, indicating the result is NOT statistically significant at α = .05. We cannot be confident that there is a genuine difference between the conditions.”

Exercise 4: Direction Error

A researcher reports: “The t-test showed that Group A scored significantly higher than Group B, t(28) = -2.45, p = .021.”

a) What potential error might be in this report?

TipShow Answer

The negative t-value (-2.45) suggests that the first group (A) actually had a lower mean than the second group (B), not higher. The researcher may have misinterpreted the direction of the effect.

Note: Whether this is an error depends on how the test was set up. If Group A was entered first and Group B second, t = -2.45 means A < B.

b) How should the researcher verify the direction?

TipShow Answer
  • Check the descriptive statistics (means) for each group
  • Look at the “sample estimates” section of the R output
  • The sign of the t-value indicates which group is higher
  • t = negative → first group has lower mean
  • t = positive → first group has higher mean

Exercise 5: P-Value Misreport

A student sees this output and reports “p < .001”:

t = 2.34, df = 18, p-value = 0.031

a) What error did the student make?

TipShow Answer

The student reported p < .001 when the actual p-value is .031 (much larger). This is a misreading of the output or confusing significant with highly significant.

The correct report should be: p = .031 (or p < .05)

b) Why does accuracy matter here?

TipShow Answer
  • p = .031 and p < .001 represent very different levels of evidence
  • p = .031 is just barely significant at .05 level
  • p < .001 suggests very strong evidence against the null
  • Misreporting could mislead readers about the strength of findings
  • Accurate reporting is essential for scientific integrity

Part 3: Degrees of Freedom Errors

Exercise 6: Wrong DF

A researcher ran a paired t-test with 15 participants but reported df = 15.

a) What is the error?

TipShow Answer

For a paired t-test, df = n - 1. With 15 participants, df should be 14, not 15.

If df = 15 is reported, either: - There were actually 16 participants, or - The wrong test was run, or - The df was misread/misreported

b) How can you verify the correct df from the output?

TipShow Answer
  • Paired t-test: df = number of pairs - 1
  • Independent t-test (Welch): df is adjusted based on sample sizes and variances
  • Standard independent t-test: df = n₁ + n₂ - 2

Cross-check against sample size in descriptives (n column).

Exercise 7: DF Suggests Wrong Test

A student reports running an independent t-test comparing 20 participants per group, with df = 19.

a) What does this suggest?

TipShow Answer

df = 19 suggests a paired test was actually run (20 - 1 = 19), not an independent test.

For an independent test with 20 per group, df should be approximately 38 (or similar with Welch’s correction).

The student either: - Ran a paired test by mistake - Misread the output - Has confused terminology

Part 4: Complex Error Scenarios

Exercise 8: Multiple Errors

Review this analysis report and identify ALL errors:

“We conducted an independent t-test to compare reaction times before and after training for the same 12 participants. Results showed t(12) = 3.45, p = .005. The 95% CI [2.1, 15.8] includes zero, indicating a non-significant result.”

List all errors:

TipShow Answer

Error 1: Wrong test type - Same participants measured before and after = should use PAIRED t-test - “Independent t-test” is wrong

Error 2: Wrong degrees of freedom - If it were a paired test with 12 participants, df should be 11 - df = 12 is wrong for either test type

Error 3: CI interpretation contradicts p-value - p = .005 indicates significant result - CI [2.1, 15.8] does NOT include zero (both values positive) - Student claims CI includes zero and result is non-significant - both wrong - Actually: CI doesn’t include zero, which is consistent with p < .05

Error 4: Contradictory conclusion - Says “non-significant” but p = .005 is clearly significant

Exercise 9: Spot the Error

A researcher reports: “A paired samples t-test compared memory scores between patients with depression (n = 25) and healthy controls (n = 25).”

What’s wrong?

TipShow Answer

Error: Wrong test for the design

This is comparing different groups of people (patients vs controls), which requires an independent samples t-test, not a paired test.

A paired test would only be appropriate if: - The same people were measured under both conditions, OR - Participants were individually matched (e.g., each patient paired with a control of same age/gender)

Unless matching was done (and not mentioned), this should be an independent t-test.

Exercise 10: Comprehensive Error Detection

Review this complete analysis:

“Hypothesis: Stress levels would be higher in exam week than in regular week.

Method: 30 students completed a stress questionnaire during exam week. A different group of 30 students completed it during a regular week.

Analysis:

t.test(exam_stress, regular_stress, paired = TRUE)
    Paired t-test
t = 2.89, df = 29, p-value = 0.007
95% percent confidence interval: 1.23, 8.77

Results: The paired t-test revealed stress was significantly higher in the exam condition (p < .05). However, since the 95% CI of [1.23, 8.77] could include zero, we cannot be fully confident in this finding.”

Identify all errors and explain the correct interpretations:

TipShow Answer

Error 1: Wrong test (Major) - Two DIFFERENT groups of students = between-subjects design - Should use independent t-test (paired = FALSE) - Paired test is completely inappropriate here

Error 2: CI misinterpretation - The CI [1.23, 8.77] does NOT include zero - Both bounds are positive - This is consistent with, not contradictory to, the significant p-value

Error 3: Contradictory reasoning - States p < .05 (significant) then expresses doubt based on CI - The CI actually supports the significant finding - Student seems confused about how CI and p-value relate

Correct interpretation would be: “An independent samples t-test should be conducted. Results would show whether exam-week students reported significantly higher stress than regular-week students. If p < .05 and CI excludes zero, we can be confident of a real difference.”

Part 5: Quick Quiz

For each scenario, identify the error type:

Scenario Error Type
Same participants, used independent t-test ?
Different groups, used paired t-test ?
CI includes zero, reported as significant ?
CI excludes zero, reported as non-significant ?
Negative t, described as Group A > Group B ?
df = 24 with 25 pairs in paired test ?
TipShow Answers
Scenario Error Type
Same participants, used independent t-test Wrong test (should be paired)
Different groups, used paired t-test Wrong test (should be independent)
CI includes zero, reported as significant CI misinterpretation
CI excludes zero, reported as non-significant CI misinterpretation
Negative t, described as Group A > Group B Direction error
df = 24 with 25 pairs in paired test Correct! (25-1=24)

Summary

After completing this self-assessment, you should be able to:

MCQ (Part A): - Identify design type, IV, DV, and correct test from a scenario - Distinguish one-tailed and two-tailed hypotheses - Interpret p-values and significance; explain correlation and causation - Define operationalisation, replication, confounding, null hypothesis, population

EDS (Part B): - Identify when paired vs independent t-tests are misapplied - Recognise misinterpretations of confidence intervals - Detect direction errors and degrees of freedom errors - Spot contradictions between statistics and interpretations

Final Preparation

ImportantBefore the MCQ (11 March)
  • Revise design → test (independent t, paired t, Pearson’s r)
  • Practise IV/DV, hypothesis type, p-value interpretation
  • Review general concepts (operationalisation, replication, confounding, null, population)

Before the EDS (Due 20 March)

  • Review all self-assessments from Weeks 12-17
  • Practise reading R output and writing APA results paragraphs
  • Know when to use paired vs independent tests; interpret CIs
  • Practise data quality (outlier, SD, sensitivity) and Section B (improvements, individual differences, ecological validity)

Good luck on the MCQ Exam and EDS Assignment!

- Misreporting could mislead readers about the strength of findings
- Accurate reporting is essential for scientific integrity
:::

---

## Part 3: Degrees of Freedom Errors

### Exercise 6: Wrong DF

A researcher ran a paired t-test with 15 participants but reported df = 15.

**a)** What is the error?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true"}
## Show Answer
For a paired t-test, df = n - 1. With 15 participants, df should be **14**, not 15.

If df = 15 is reported, either:
- There were actually 16 participants, or
- The wrong test was run, or
- The df was misread/misreported
:::

**b)** How can you verify the correct df from the output?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true"}
## Show Answer
- Paired t-test: df = number of pairs - 1
- Independent t-test (Welch): df is adjusted based on sample sizes and variances
- Standard independent t-test: df = n₁ + n₂ - 2

Cross-check against sample size in descriptives (n column).
:::

---

### Exercise 7: DF Suggests Wrong Test

A student reports running an independent t-test comparing 20 participants per group, with df = 19.

**a)** What does this suggest?

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true"}
## Show Answer
df = 19 suggests a **paired test** was actually run (20 - 1 = 19), not an independent test.

For an independent test with 20 per group, df should be approximately 38 (or similar with Welch's correction).

The student either:
- Ran a paired test by mistake
- Misread the output
- Has confused terminology
:::

## Part 4: Complex Error Scenarios

### Exercise 8: Multiple Errors

Review this analysis report and identify ALL errors:

> "We conducted an independent t-test to compare reaction times before and after training for the same 12 participants. Results showed t(12) = 3.45, p = .005. The 95% CI [2.1, 15.8] includes zero, indicating a non-significant result."

**List all errors:**

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true"}
## Show Answer
**Error 1: Wrong test type**
- Same participants measured before and after = should use PAIRED t-test
- "Independent t-test" is wrong

**Error 2: Wrong degrees of freedom**
- If it were a paired test with 12 participants, df should be 11
- df = 12 is wrong for either test type

**Error 3: CI interpretation contradicts p-value**
- p = .005 indicates significant result
- CI [2.1, 15.8] does NOT include zero (both values positive)
- Student claims CI includes zero and result is non-significant - both wrong
- Actually: CI doesn't include zero, which is consistent with p < .05

**Error 4: Contradictory conclusion**
- Says "non-significant" but p = .005 is clearly significant
:::

---

### Exercise 9: Spot the Error

A researcher reports: "A paired samples t-test compared memory scores between patients with depression (n = 25) and healthy controls (n = 25)."

**What's wrong?**

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true"}
## Show Answer
**Error: Wrong test for the design**

This is comparing **different groups of people** (patients vs controls), which requires an **independent samples t-test**, not a paired test.

A paired test would only be appropriate if:
- The same people were measured under both conditions, OR
- Participants were individually matched (e.g., each patient paired with a control of same age/gender)

Unless matching was done (and not mentioned), this should be an independent t-test.
:::

### Exercise 10: Comprehensive Error Detection

Review this complete analysis:

> "Hypothesis: Stress levels would be higher in exam week than in regular week.
>
> Method: 30 students completed a stress questionnaire during exam week. A different group of 30 students completed it during a regular week.
>
> Analysis:
> ```
> t.test(exam_stress, regular_stress, paired = TRUE)
>     Paired t-test
> t = 2.89, df = 29, p-value = 0.007
> 95% percent confidence interval: 1.23, 8.77
> ```
>
> Results: The paired t-test revealed stress was significantly higher in the exam condition (p < .05). However, since the 95% CI of [1.23, 8.77] could include zero, we cannot be fully confident in this finding."

**Identify all errors and explain the correct interpretations:**

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true"}
## Show Answer
**Error 1: Wrong test (Major)**
- Two DIFFERENT groups of students = between-subjects design
- Should use independent t-test (`paired = FALSE`)
- Paired test is completely inappropriate here

**Error 2: CI misinterpretation**
- The CI [1.23, 8.77] does NOT include zero
- Both bounds are positive
- This is consistent with, not contradictory to, the significant p-value

**Error 3: Contradictory reasoning**
- States p < .05 (significant) then expresses doubt based on CI
- The CI actually supports the significant finding
- Student seems confused about how CI and p-value relate

**Correct interpretation would be:**
"An independent samples t-test should be conducted. Results would show whether exam-week students reported significantly higher stress than regular-week students. If p < .05 and CI excludes zero, we can be confident of a real difference."
:::
