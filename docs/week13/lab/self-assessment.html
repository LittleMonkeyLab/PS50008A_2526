Week 13 Self-Assessment

Reading R Correlation Output

Author

Gordon Wright

Published

28 January 2026

Overview

This self-assessment consolidates your learning from Week 13 and builds on your R output interpretation skills by focusing on correlation output.

NoteFormative Assessment

This self-assessment is not graded but completing it will prepare you for: - The EDS Assignment (interpreting R output) - The Research Report (correlation analysis)

Part 1: Reading Correlation Output

Exercise 1: Basic Correlation Output

A researcher examined the relationship between study hours and exam scores. Here’s the R output:

> cor.test(data$study_hours, data$exam_score)

    Pearson's product-moment correlation

data:  data$study_hours and data$exam_score
t = 4.23, df = 28, p-value = 0.0002
alternative hypothesis: true correlation is not equal to 0
95 percent confidence interval:
 0.36 0.79
sample estimates:
      cor
0.624

a) What is the correlation coefficient?

TipShow Answer

r = 0.624 (shown in the “sample estimates” section as “cor”).

b) Is this correlation statistically significant at α = .05?

TipShow Answer

Yes, it is statistically significant because p = 0.0002, which is less than .05.

c) How would you describe the strength and direction of this relationship?

TipShow Answer

This is a moderate to strong positive correlation. As study hours increase, exam scores tend to increase.

  • Direction: Positive (both variables move in the same direction)
  • Strength: Moderate-strong (r = 0.624 is between 0.5 and 0.7)

d) Report this correlation in APA format.

TipShow Answer

There was a significant positive correlation between study hours and exam scores, r(28) = .62, p < .001, 95% CI [.36, .79].

Or:

Study hours were significantly positively correlated with exam scores, r = .62, p < .001.

Exercise 2: Interpreting the Confidence Interval

Using the output from Exercise 1:

95 percent confidence interval:
 0.36 0.79

a) What does this confidence interval tell us?

TipShow Answer

We are 95% confident that the true population correlation falls between 0.36 and 0.79. The interval is entirely positive, confirming the positive relationship.

b) Why is it important that this interval doesn’t include zero?

TipShow Answer

If the interval included zero, we couldn’t rule out the possibility of no relationship. Since it doesn’t include zero, we have evidence of a genuine correlation in the population.

Exercise 3: Different Correlation Strengths

Match each correlation coefficient to its description:

Coefficient Options
r = -0.89 A. Weak positive
r = 0.23 B. Strong negative
r = 0.52 C. Moderate positive
r = -0.15 D. Weak negative
TipShow Answer
  • r = -0.89 → B. Strong negative
  • r = 0.23 → A. Weak positive
  • r = 0.52 → C. Moderate positive
  • r = -0.15 → D. Weak negative

Part 2: Descriptive Statistics for Correlational Studies

Exercise 4: Reading Paired Descriptives

A study measured anxiety and sleep quality in 50 participants:

> data %>%
+   summarise(
+     mean_anxiety = mean(anxiety),
+     sd_anxiety = sd(anxiety),
+     mean_sleep = mean(sleep_quality),
+     sd_sleep = sd(sleep_quality)
+   )

# A tibble: 1 x 4
  mean_anxiety sd_anxiety mean_sleep sd_sleep
         <dbl>      <dbl>      <dbl>    <dbl>
1         42.3       12.1       5.8      1.9

a) Report the descriptive statistics for anxiety in APA format.

TipShow Answer

Anxiety scores ranged from… (M = 42.3, SD = 12.1)

Or simply: Mean anxiety was 42.3 (SD = 12.1).

b) What additional information would you need to fully interpret these descriptives?

TipShow Answer

You would benefit from knowing: - The range of the anxiety scale (e.g., 0-100) - What higher/lower scores mean (is high anxiety bad?) - The range of the sleep quality scale - Sample size (n)

Exercise 5: Correlation with Full Descriptives

Here’s complete output for a study on age and memory performance:

> # Descriptive statistics
> data %>%
+   summarise(
+     n = n(),
+     mean_age = mean(age),
+     sd_age = sd(age),
+     mean_memory = mean(memory_score),
+     sd_memory = sd(memory_score)
+   )

# A tibble: 1 x 5
      n mean_age sd_age mean_memory sd_memory
  <int>    <dbl>  <dbl>       <dbl>     <dbl>
1    45     52.4   15.3        68.2      18.7

> # Correlation test
> cor.test(data$age, data$memory_score)

    Pearson's product-moment correlation

data:  data$age and data$memory_score
t = -3.87, df = 43, p-value = 0.0004
alternative hypothesis: true correlation is not equal to 0
95 percent confidence interval:
 -0.68 -0.24
sample estimates:
       cor
-0.508

a) Write a complete results paragraph in APA format.

TipShow Answer

A total of 45 participants (mean age = 52.4 years, SD = 15.3) completed a memory task (M = 68.2, SD = 18.7). There was a significant negative correlation between age and memory performance, r(43) = -.51, p < .001, 95% CI [-.68, -.24], indicating that older participants tended to show lower memory scores.

b) What is the coefficient of determination (r²) and what does it tell us?

TipShow Answer

r² = (-0.508)² = 0.258 = 25.8%

This tells us that approximately 26% of the variance in memory scores can be explained by (or is shared with) age. The remaining 74% is due to other factors.

Part 3: Correlation vs Causation

Exercise 6: Critical Thinking

A researcher finds a significant correlation (r = .65) between ice cream sales and drowning deaths.

a) Can we conclude that ice cream causes drowning? Why or why not?

TipShow Answer

No, correlation does not imply causation. Both variables are likely influenced by a third variable (confound): hot weather. Hot weather increases both ice cream sales AND swimming (which increases drowning risk).

b) What type of variable is “hot weather” in this example?

TipShow Answer

Hot weather is a confounding variable (or third variable). It affects both the IV and DV, creating a spurious correlation.

Exercise 7: Identifying Confounds

For each correlation, identify a potential confounding variable:

a) Shoe size correlates with reading ability in children.

TipShow Answer

Age is the confound. Older children have bigger feet AND better reading ability.

b) Number of firefighters at a scene correlates with property damage.

TipShow Answer

Fire size/severity is the confound. Larger fires require more firefighters AND cause more damage.

Part 4: Design Recognition

Exercise 8: Study Design

A researcher wants to know if there’s a relationship between hours of sleep and exam performance among university students. They survey 100 students, asking about their typical sleep duration and collecting their most recent exam grade.

a) What type of design is this?

TipShow Answer

This is a correlational design (also called observational or non-experimental). The researcher is not manipulating any variables, just measuring the natural relationship between two variables.

b) What are two variables being measured?

TipShow Answer
  1. Hours of sleep (typical/habitual)
  2. Exam performance/grade

c) Why can’t we conclude that sleep causes better exam performance?

TipShow Answer
  • Variables are not manipulated (no random assignment)
  • Cannot rule out confounding variables (e.g., conscientiousness affects both sleep habits and studying)
  • Direction of causality unclear (maybe good grades reduce stress → better sleep)
  • Would need an experiment to establish causation

Summary

After completing this self-assessment, you should be able to:

  • Read and interpret R correlation output
  • Extract r, df, p-value, and confidence interval from cor.test() output
  • Report correlations in APA format
  • Calculate and interpret r²
  • Identify confounding variables
  • Explain why correlation ≠ causation

  • Independent t-test output from R
  • When to use independent vs paired tests
  • Confidence intervals for mean differences
