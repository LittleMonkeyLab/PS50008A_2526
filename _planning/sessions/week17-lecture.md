## Outcomes
C17, C18, R5

## One-liner
Why everything we've taught them is routinely abused: p-hacking, HARKing, the replication crisis, and how to do better.

## Paragraph


## Structure
- 0-10 min: "We ran 20 correlations on our Data Olympics data. How many significant by chance?"
- 10-25 min: P-hacking and researcher degrees of freedom [C17]—examples, demonstrations
- 25-35 min: HARKing, publication bias, file drawer problem
- 35-45 min: The replication crisis—what happened, why it matters
- 45-50 min: How to do better—preregistration, effect sizes [C18], CIs, honest reporting

## Key points for MCQ
- Multiple comparisons inflate false positive rate
- "Significant" doesn't mean "important" or "true"
- Effect sizes complement (don't replace) p-values [C18]

## Examples and demos
- Live demo: run all pairwise correlations on Data Olympics, highlight "significant" ones
- Show garden of forking paths
- Real replication failure examples (ego depletion, power poses)

## Connections
- To lab: Practice critiquing published work
- To project: Students should report honestly, include effect sizes
- To prior weeks: Synthesises everything—now they see why rigour matters
- To future weeks: Week 18 MCQ; week 20 EDS Section B requires methods critique

## Materials needed
- Data Olympics full correlation matrix

## Pedagogy notes
This can feel cynical—balance criticism with "here's how to do it right." Students often feel anxious about "doing it wrong." Emphasise that awareness is the first step. The Data Olympics multiple comparisons demo should be a gut-punch moment.
